# AI Fashion Aggregator - B2B / Affiliate / Partnership Notes

## Core Strategic Direction

The project should initially position itself as:

- AI fashion discovery engine
- semantic fashion search platform
- fashion aggregation/search layer
- traffic/referral source for stores

NOT as:

- reseller
- dropshipping operation
- centralized ecommerce store
- warehouse/logistics business

---

# Key Strategic Insight

The strongest early positioning is:

> "We bring highly targeted fashion-intent traffic to stores."

This framing is significantly safer and more scalable than acting as a hidden reseller or trying to own the checkout process from day one.

---

Most Lithuanian fashion stores have:

- weak search systems
- poor filtering
- weak SEO
- low discoverability
- outdated mobile UX
- no semantic search
- no AI recommendation layer

The aggregator can function as an external AI-powered discovery layer.

Example:

> "washed oversized zip hoodie with stars"

The aggregator becomes better at understanding fashion intent than the stores themselves.

# Risk 1 - Product Data / Legal / Store Relationship Risk

## Why This Risk Matters

The biggest early risk is not the search UI. It is the right to collect, store, enrich, display, and monetize product data from stores.

Fashion product data can include:

- product names
- descriptions
- prices
- stock/size availability
- product images
- brand names
- category structure
- store URLs
- metadata extracted from product pages

Even if product pages are publicly visible, that does not automatically mean the platform can freely copy, store, reuse, enrich, and monetize all product data at scale.

The risk depends heavily on the data acquisition method.

---

## Main Risk Areas

### Store Terms And Conditions

Some stores may prohibit scraping, automated access, copying product content, commercial reuse, or use of images.

Before indexing a store, check:

- website terms
- robots.txt
- sitemap behavior
- affiliate/program terms
- image/content usage terms
- anti-bot or rate-limit behavior

If the store explicitly blocks automated access or commercial reuse, do not use it for the MVP without permission.

### Product Images And Descriptions

Product photos and descriptions are usually the most sensitive content.

Risks:

- images may be owned by the store, brand, photographer, or supplier
- product descriptions may be copyrighted
- storing and redisplaying full descriptions may create avoidable IP risk
- large-scale copying of images can make the platform look like an unauthorized marketplace

Safer MVP approach:

- use store-provided feeds or permission where possible
- display only necessary product information
- link clearly to the original store
- avoid rewriting or representing store content as owned by the aggregator
- avoid copying long descriptions unless licensed/approved

### EU Database Rights

In the EU, databases can receive separate protection if there was substantial investment in obtaining, verifying, or presenting the data.

This matters because extracting or reusing a substantial part of a store's catalog can create risk even when individual fields are simple facts.

Practical implication:

- scraping a few public product pages for testing is lower risk
- copying a store's full catalog repeatedly is higher risk
- partner feeds, affiliate feeds, APIs, or written permission are much safer

### Brand And Trademark Presentation

The platform must not imply official partnership unless there is one.

Safer language:

- "Available at Store X"
- "View in store"
- "Product data from Store X"

Avoid:

- "official partner" unless true
- using brand logos without permission
- presenting the platform as the seller
- confusing users about who handles payment, delivery, and returns

### Price And Availability Accuracy

If prices or stock are stale, users lose trust and stores may complain.

Minimum requirements:

- show "last updated" internally, and possibly publicly later
- update product data regularly
- handle out-of-stock products
- send users to the store for final price, stock, shipping, and checkout
- avoid promising that aggregator data is final

### Technical Access And Rate Limits

Aggressive scraping can damage relationships and trigger blocking.

Minimum rules:

- respect robots.txt
- use low request rates
- identify the crawler where appropriate
- avoid bypassing anti-bot systems
- stop crawling if blocked
- prefer sitemaps, feeds, APIs, and partner files

## Data Acquisition Methods

### Method A - Direct Store Partnership / Product Feed

Lowest risk and best long-term method.

The store provides:

- CSV/XML/JSON feed
- API access
- Shopify/WooCommerce export
- product image permission
- referral tracking agreement
- update frequency expectations

Best for:

- serious MVP
- later monetization
- store trust
- reliable product data
- clean image/content rights

Store selection criteria:

- store is small/medium and can make decisions quickly
- store has enough SKUs to make search useful
- store has weak native search/filtering
- store wants more traffic
- store is open to affiliate/referral tracking
- store can provide product data or approve crawling
- store has stable product URLs
- store has decent product images
- store sells products relevant to the target style/aesthetic users

Recommended first outreach angle:

> "We are building an AI fashion discovery layer. We can send users directly to your product pages and share search/click analytics. We do not handle checkout or resell your products."

### Method B - Affiliate Network / Existing Product Feed

Low-to-medium risk if terms clearly allow product feed usage.

The platform joins an affiliate program or network that provides:

- product feeds
- tracking links
- commission terms
- permitted promotional methods
- image/content usage permissions

Best for:

- faster monetization
- stores already comfortable with affiliate traffic
- cleaner attribution

Watch out for:

- feed freshness
- restrictions on SEO pages
- restrictions on bidding/brand keywords
- restrictions on image usage
- restrictions on modifying descriptions
- country-specific commission coverage

Store selection criteria:

- affiliate terms allow catalog/search use
- feed includes images, prices, availability, sizes, and product URLs
- commission is not the only reason to include the store
- products match the target market
- feed quality is high enough for semantic tagging

### Method C - Public Indexing / Controlled Crawling

Higher risk. Useful for validation, but should be limited and careful.

This means indexing public product pages without a formal partnership.

Only consider stores where:

- robots.txt does not block relevant pages
- terms do not clearly prohibit commercial indexing/reuse
- product pages are stable and public
- request volume can stay low
- the platform links back prominently
- the store is likely to benefit from referral traffic

For this method, store only what is needed:

- product title
- price
- canonical URL
- small set of extracted attributes
- image URL or cached image only if legally acceptable
- internal AI tags generated by the platform

Avoid:

- copying full catalogs at high frequency
- copying full product descriptions
- bypassing anti-bot systems
- implying partnership
- using blocked stores
- caching large image sets without permission

This method should be treated as a bridge to partnership, not the final operating model.

### Method D - Manual Curated Catalog

Lowest technical complexity, but not scalable.

This means manually adding selected products from stores for a prototype or demo.

Best for:

- testing search UX
- testing tagging quality
- building a pitch demo
- avoiding heavy crawling before store conversations

Limitations:

- not scalable
- data becomes stale quickly
- still needs caution around images/content
- not enough to prove automated aggregation

# Store Selection Strategy

## The Answer: It Depends On The Method

Yes, store selection depends strongly on how product data will be obtained.

If using direct partnerships, choose stores based on responsiveness, data access, and willingness to test.

If using affiliate feeds, choose stores based on feed quality, terms, tracking reliability, and category fit.

If using public indexing, choose stores based on legal/technical permissibility, robots/terms, stable public product pages, and low relationship risk.

Do not start by asking:

> "Which stores are the biggest?"

Start by asking:

> "Which stores can we safely and reliably index while proving that semantic fashion search creates referral value?"

# Recommended Data Strategy For MVP

## Best Practical Path

1. Build prototype search with a small manually curated or carefully indexed dataset.
2. Use it to demonstrate semantic search quality.
3. Approach 5-10 small/medium stores with a simple partnership pitch.
4. Prefer stores that can provide CSV/API/export access.
5. Add affiliate/referral tracking only after product/search quality works.
6. Use public crawling only as a limited fallback and bridge to permission-based feeds.

The safest long-term model is:

> Permission-based product feeds + referral tracking + analytics for stores.

The riskiest long-term model is:

> Large-scale scraping + copied images/descriptions + monetization without store permission.

# Strategic Pivot - Large Retailer Catalog First

## Updated Direction

If the target user expects ASOS, Zalando, Zara, Bershka, H&M, Mango, Pull&Bear, Stradivarius, Farfetch, About You, and similar retailers, then the MVP should not be built primarily around small Lithuanian boutiques.

Small local stores are useful for:

- early partnerships
- local differentiation
- Baltic expansion story
- direct B2B outreach
- testing store analytics

But they are usually weak as the core B2C catalog because:

- catalog depth is limited
- product velocity is lower
- brand recognition is weaker
- users may not immediately trust unknown stores
- size/color availability may be thinner
- style coverage is narrower

For a consumer-facing MVP, large retailers are more useful because they provide:

- recognizable brands
- deep catalogs
- fast trend coverage
- broad price ranges
- size/color variants
- frequent new arrivals
- strong user intent
- better SEO opportunity

The revised product strategy should be:

> Build the B2C discovery experience on large retailer affiliate/product feeds, then add Lithuanian/local stores as a curated differentiation layer.

---

## Critical Rule

Large retailers should be treated as feed/API/affiliate-first targets, not scraping-first targets.

Do not build the MVP around unauthorized scraping of ASOS/Zara/Bershka/H&M-style sites.

Reasons:

- terms often prohibit automated extraction, commercial reuse, or copying
- product images and editorial content are strongly protected
- anti-bot systems can block or degrade access
- legal teams are more active than with small stores
- affiliate approval can be lost if traffic methods violate terms
- price/stock freshness matters more at scale

The safe path is:

> Affiliate network approval + product feed access + compliant deep links + clear attribution.

## What Product Feeds Solve

Affiliate product feeds are a much better primitive than crawlers for this MVP.

They can provide:

- product ID
- title
- brand
- category
- price
- sale price
- currency
- product URL
- affiliate tracking URL
- image URL
- availability/stock
- size/color fields depending on retailer/feed quality
- update timestamps depending on network/feed

This is exactly what an AI fashion aggregator needs.

Feed-first architecture also makes the platform more defensible:

- fewer legal questions
- cleaner attribution
- easier monetization
- easier merchant/network compliance
- easier product refresh
- no need to bypass anti-bot systems

## Phase 4 - Feed Import Pipeline

Goal:

Import approved XML/CSV feeds safely, repeatedly, and with quality checks.

Pipeline steps:

1. Fetch feed
   - use network-provided feed URL/API/export
   - never crawl merchant pages as a substitute for feed access
   - store import run metadata

2. Parse feed
   - support XML first because VIV pages confirm XML feeds
   - support CSV/TSV next for Awin or other networks
   - preserve raw payload in raw_feed_items

3. Validate required fields
   - external product id
   - title
   - product URL or deeplink target
   - image URL
   - price
   - currency
   - availability or stock signal if present
   - category
   - brand if present

4. Normalize
   - brand casing
   - category taxonomy
   - gender
   - color
   - sizes
   - price and old price
   - availability states

5. Generate affiliate URLs
   - prefer feed-provided affiliate/deeplink URL
   - otherwise use network deeplink builder only if approved
   - attach subid/click reference for analytics

6. Upsert products
   - match by store_id + external_id
   - update only if source_hash changed
   - keep first_seen_at and last_seen_at

7. Handle disappeared items
   - do not delete immediately
   - mark as possibly_out_of_stock after first missed import
   - mark out_of_stock after 2-3 missed imports
   - hide from search if out_of_stock

8. Enrichment trigger
   - generate embeddings only for new or changed products
   - do not re-embed unchanged feed rows
   - keep embedding_input_hash to control AI costs

9. QA report
   - count missing images
   - count missing prices
   - count duplicate URLs
   - count category mapping failures
   - count broken product links if checked

Exit criteria:

- first approved feed imported end-to-end
- import can be rerun idempotently
- failed rows do not break whole import
- QA report is saved for every import

## Unit Economics Reality Check

Before spending money on ads, calculate the value of a paid visitor.

Formula:

Revenue per site visitor =

site clickout rate x merchant conversion rate x average order value x commission rate x validation rate

Example:

- site clickout rate: 25%
- merchant conversion rate after clickout: 2%
- average order value: EUR 50
- affiliate commission: 7%
- validation rate after returns/rejections: 80%

Calculation:

0.25 x 0.02 x 50 x 0.07 x 0.80 = EUR 0.014 per site visitor

Interpretation:

If a paid ad click costs EUR 0.10-0.50, this loses money badly as direct arbitrage.

This is why paid ads should initially be used for:

- testing which queries users care about
- testing landing page conversion
- collecting search terms
- proving there is clickout behavior
- building retargeting audiences
- improving affiliate approval credibility

Paid ads should not be treated as the main profit engine until real EPC and conversion data prove otherwise.

- dedicated worker infrastructure
- external search service
- large paid ad campaigns
- merchant image caching
- multi-country localization

Reason:

Without feed approval, these can become wasted work or force the product into scraping/mocked-data behavior.

---

## Best Pre-Application Build Scope

Build this:

1. Landing page
2. Demo search page
3. Mock product grid
4. Policy pages
5. Store/data source page
6. Minimal DB schema or migration files
7. Clickout route skeleton
8. Store application tracker
9. Basic analytics/click event model
10. Deployment pipeline

Do not build more until at least 2 stores are approved.

---

## Suggested 3-Day Pre-Application Sprint

### Day 1 - Public Trust Layer

- choose project name/domain
- deploy site shell
- write homepage copy
- add About/Contact
- add Privacy/Terms/Affiliate Disclosure/Data Source Policy

### Day 2 - Demo Product Experience

- build visual search/demo page
- create mock product cards
- add search/filter UI
- add stores/data sources page
- add clear explanation of approved-feed model

### Day 3 - Feed-Ready Skeleton

- create DB schema/migration
- create store tracker
- create `/out/:productId` redirect skeleton
- add search/click event structure
- prepare application texts and screenshots

After Day 3:

- apply to Reserved LT
- apply to Sinsay LT
- apply to Sizeer LT
- apply to MODIVO LT

---

## Pre-Application Infrastructure Definition Of Done

Ready to apply when:

- site is live
- site explains the model clearly
- legal/trust pages exist
- demo search works with mock/owned data
- no unapproved merchant catalog is shown as live
- store tracker exists
- application texts are ready
- technical model is feed-first
- clickout tracking route is planned or stubbed
- no paid ad claims or brand SEM claims create compliance risk

Recommended mindset:

> Build enough infrastructure to be trusted and ready. Do not build enough to get trapped polishing a product with no approved data.
